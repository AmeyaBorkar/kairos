`incremental` is recovery minus what the do-nothing arm collected. It is the column no dunning dashboard shows, and
the gap between it and `recovered` is customers who were coming back regardless. `wasted` counts the actions that landed
on exactly those people. `true cost` adds the priced value of every customer lost to an opt-out, which is the cost that
dominates and appears on no invoice.

The baselines are given every advantage that is not the point of the comparison: the same Terminus mandate, the same
budget, the same contact cap, and a message deferred rather than dropped when it lands in quiet hours. What they do not
get is Kairos's classification — a naive ladder has not classified anything, so it sends generic copy and earns none of
the stopping rules that come from knowing what went wrong.

Kairos recovered ₹17,71,959.00 gross, ₹6,32,030.00 of it incremental, for a true cost of ₹11,496.33.
Against the fixed ladder: 1.4% less incremental recovery, 65.6% lower true cost, 52.9% fewer messages,
and 101 fewer customers lost.

The honest headline is not that Kairos recovers more. A ladder that messages every casualty three times recovers a
little more money than one that thinks about it — brute force works. It works by sending twice the messages and
costing 155 customers their consent rather than 54, and the merchant pays for that in a currency
that does not appear on the invoice.

`legible` is the share of messages that arrived in a script the recipient reads. For every template arm it is simply the English share of the population, because a template is English whatever it is asked for — that gap is the problem the library was built to close, and it is reported rather than assumed.

Generated copy recovered ₹64,648.00 more than the same system running templates (10.2%), on 126 fewer messages and 11 fewer opt-outs.
Fewer messages is not a separate saving, it is the same effect seen from the other end: a customer who acts on the first message never receives the second.

Each row is the mean of 4 seeds, and the two right-hand columns are the best and worst of them. They are wide on purpose: a single-seed version of this table came out non-monotonic, because seed-to-seed variation on this metric is 4.18% and the gap between adjacent rows is smaller than that.

The row that matters is the last one. At a penalty of 1.00 a message in a script the reader does not use works exactly as well as one they do, the entire multilingual argument is void, and whatever gain remains is the library naming the rail rather than speaking the language. Nobody has measured where on this table the truth sits: it is open question 18, and ADR 0007 spends real postage on the strength of it.

120 min  ₹6,26,109.00  5578      249     540    61  ₹12,963.67

Waiting 120 minutes instead of none sends 14.9% fewer messages, wastes 49.1% fewer of them on customers who were
already returning, and recovers 20.7% more incremental revenue.

The window went in on the argument that a nudge ninety seconds after a cancelled payment is mostly paid for by people who
were already reaching for another card, and that waiting therefore trades recovery for restraint. The table says there is no
trade. Messages and wasted actions fall monotonically with the window and incremental recovery does not fall with them,
because the two mechanisms point the same way: a customer who returns unaided closes their own casualty and never costs a
message, and a transient casualty asked later is asked when its rail is likelier to have healed. Waiting is not the price of
restraint here — it is most of the product.

The 45-minute default was chosen before this table existed and the sweep suggests it is too short. Opt-out counts across the
sweep are noisy enough that the cheapest row is not stable, so the default is left where it is rather than tuned to one run.
